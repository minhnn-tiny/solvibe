## Summary

The proposal studies agent-written replacements for audited Solidity functions, using probability sampling across audit-flagged and unflagged code, executable vulnerability confirmation, and a factorial experiment on faulty assertions and execution access.

I found substantial prior art for its components, but no verified paper duplicating the complete proposed comparison. The strongest remaining contribution is the population-level measurement design. The faulty-test experiment has a narrower novelty margin than the proposal’s emphasis suggests.

My independent search covered arXiv, publisher pages, OpenReview, and venue/programme searches spanning 2024–2027. It identified additional relevant work beyond the supplied literature synthesis. Coverage is not exhaustive: some IEEE, USENIX, and OpenReview pages were inaccessible, and coverage of forthcoming 2027 work remains [uncertain].

## Strengths

- Sampling unflagged functions addresses a real limitation of benchmarks selected around historical vulnerabilities.
- The proposal carefully distinguishes confirmed vulnerability absence, functional failure, compilation failure, and unresolved evaluation.
- Target-only correction, legitimate-behavior checks, and common exploit replay provide considerably stronger evidence than static-warning counts.
- The threat model and disclosure procedures are explicit.
- The authors acknowledge historical-context asymmetry, incomplete detection, synthetic-test limitations, and prior art rather than claiming a new repair technique.

## Weaknesses

1. **The principal novelty is plausible, but its empirical value is not directly tested.**

   **Evidence:** Section 3 identifies “the weighted comparison that includes unflagged functions” as a distinct question. However, the planned analysis does not explicitly measure how conclusions change when using the historical-vulnerability-only design that motivates the project.

   The closest overlaps are:

   - **“SecureVibeBench: Benchmarking Secure Vibe Coding of AI Agents via Reconstructing Vulnerability-Introducing Scenarios,” ACL 2026:** overlaps in reconstructing developer tasks, comparing agent outputs against historical human vulnerabilities, and combining functional and security evaluation. It does not establish the proposed weighted unflagged-function comparison. [Opened paper page](https://aclanthology.org/2026.acl-long.1107/).
   - **“How Safe Are AI-Generated Patches? A Large-scale Study on Security Risks in LLM and Agentic Automated Program Repair on SWE-bench,” arXiv preprint, 2025:** already studies human and agent patches for the same issues and newly introduced security problems. Consequently, detecting vulnerabilities beyond a historical target bug is not itself a new research question. [Opened abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.02976).
   - **“EVMbench: Evaluating AI Agents on Smart Contract Security,” ICML 2026:** overlaps in executable smart-contract security evaluation, including patching and exploitation; it does not supply the proposed author comparison. [Opened proceedings page](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v306/wang26k.html).

   **Required change:** Preregister a paired design-comparison analysis reporting how the V-only estimate and agent rankings differ from the weighted V+N estimate on the same collected data, with uncertainty for those differences.

2. **RQ2 can establish its stated interaction, but a positive result may add little beyond established harmful-test-feedback findings.**

   **Evidence:** Section 6.4 crosses faulty/corrected assertions with execution permission. This is a useful intervention, but the surrounding claim needs a sharper distinction from existing work:

   - **“ImpossibleBench: Measuring LLMs’ Propensity of Exploiting Test Cases,” ICLR 2026:** constructs specification–test conflicts and varies test access and feedback. The overlap is the experimental manipulation of conflicting tests and agent access, although its outcome is specification-violating shortcuts rather than attributable Solidity vulnerabilities. [Opened full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.20270v1).
   - **“On the Risks of using LLM-Generated Tests for Regression Testing,” arXiv preprint, 2026:** already identifies and measures tests that assert and preserve faulty behavior. The overlap is the fault-enforcing-test construct and persistence claim, not the proposed execution-access interaction. [Opened abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.11835).
   - **“ExecCritic: Learn to Test, Test to Improve for Coding Agents,” arXiv preprint, 2026:** independently found in my search and absent from the supplied synthesis; explicitly addresses incorrect behavioral targets in generated tests and uses independently constructed, frozen tests to guide repair. The overlap is the problem of misleading executable feedback and separation of repair from validation. [Opened full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.09133v1).
   - **“Calibration, Not Compilation: Detecting and Repairing Misspecified Probabilistic Programs Written by Language Models,” arXiv preprint, 2026:** also independently found beyond the synthesis; reports unit-test feedback performing worse than no feedback in repair. This overlaps with the general claim that execution feedback can reinforce an incorrect solution, but concerns statistical misspecification rather than security. [Opened abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.31630).

   None establishes the exact proposed security interaction. Nevertheless, “bad tests can make feedback harmful” is already occupied territory.

   **Required change:** Add a bounded diagnostic control using incorrect assertions about non-security behavior, matched within tasks where feasible, to test whether the observed effect specifically preserves the vulnerable behavior or reflects generic compliance with incorrect tests.

3. **The generation baselines omit the closest domain-specific system despite discussing it as related work.**

   **Evidence:** Sections 5.9 and 6.7 prioritize six general-agent integrations and supported native agents. **“SolAgent: A Specialized Multi-Agent Framework for Solidity Code Generation,” arXiv preprint, 2026**, already combines Solidity generation, Foundry feedback, and Slither-guided refinement and compares generated code with human implementations. Its overlap is both the application problem and the human-versus-generated security comparison; its static-warning endpoint differs materially from this proposal’s executable confirmation. [Opened full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.23009v1).

   Testing this system with the proposed stronger oracle could produce a more informative result than another translated general-agent configuration. Its original file-level task differs from this proposal’s function replacement, so adaptation must be explicit.

   **Required change:** Replace one lower-priority experimental integration with a documented SolAgent adaptation on the common RQ1 comparison subset, evaluated under the same replacement restrictions and executable outcomes.

4. **The novel part of the benchmark is also its least demonstrated construction workload.**

   **Evidence:** Section 5.1 says the eight pilot cases were selected partly for convenient PoVs. Section 5.12 reports only two control functions, and notes that pilot artifacts still require revised attribution and acceptance checks. Yet the main design allocates eight N functions per protocol across 25–30 protocols. These cases require security properties and meaningful negative evaluations without a historical finding supplying the oracle.

   Section 8 candidly states that the pilot does not establish the labor cost of invariant harnesses, attribution, symmetric discovery, or replay. A future funding gate is prudent, but does not yet demonstrate first-year feasibility.

   **Required change:** Complete a timed end-to-end feasibility pilot on a probability-selected set of unflagged functions, retaining construction failures, and use its measured throughput to specify the minimum deliverable achievable within twelve months.

## Ratings

C1: concern — The weighted population comparison appears distinct, but most component techniques and the general faulty-feedback insight are established.

C2: pass — Audited Solidity vulnerabilities and developer adoption provide a credible practical motivation without requiring AI-specific incident attribution.

C3: pass — Sampling, executable attribution, controlled interventions, and independent validation go substantially beyond applying an LLM to a task.

C4: pass — Claims are generally bounded appropriately, including explicit limits on historical comparisons and undetected vulnerabilities.

C5: pass — Attacker powers, environmental assumptions, protected assets, exclusions, and attribution requirements are explicit.

C6: concern — The evaluation is detailed, but lacks a direct test of the sampling contribution and the closest Solidity-specific generation baseline.

C7: concern — Construction and validation of the large unflagged stratum remain insufficiently demonstrated for a first-year student’s twelve-month project.

C8: pass — Offline execution, deployment-exposure checks, disclosure, embargoes, and paid expert review are addressed.

## Questions for authors

- What conclusion would the weighted population study enable that the V-only comparison would get materially wrong?
- If faulty-test effects are similar for security and ordinary functional errors, what is the intended novel RQ2 insight?
- What measured completion rate for unflagged-function construction would trigger a reduction in scope?

## Overall score

Score: 3

Borderline. I found no decisive duplicate, but the proposal needs to demonstrate the value and feasibility of its remaining novelty. The direct V-only versus V+N analysis and a successful timed unflagged-function pilot would materially raise my score.

Confidence: 3

## Same technique, same problem, same claim?

**No.** Among the papers verified in this review, none combines probability-weighted sampling of flagged and unflagged Solidity functions, paired historical-human replacement outcomes, executable target attribution, and the proposed faulty-assertion/execution-access experiment. Individual components overlap extensively; this finding does not establish that their combination alone is a sufficient contribution.
