- **1. The in-range requirement for a short leg can fall below the leg's real loss, so wide short legs are under-collateralized**
  - **Root cause:** line 1477 to 1483 builds `r2` as `mulDivRoundingUp(amountMoved * (DECIMALS - baseCollateralRatio), (scaleFactor - ratio), DECIMALS * (scaleFactor + FP96)) + r0`. It adds `r0` (half the base requirement, line 1459 `r0 = required / 2`), not the full base `required`. The comment says `r2` is a straight line between the requirement at `tickLower` and the requirement at `tickUpper`. At the in-the-money edge, the line ends exactly `r0` below `r1` (line 1463 to 1466). For example, at the upper edge of a call, `r1 = amountMoved*(1 - 0.8/sf)`, but `r2 = amountMoved*(0.9 - 0.8/sf)`, where `sf = getSqrtRatioAtTick(tickUpper - tickLower)`. When the range covers a price factor above about 400 (sf > 20, roughly 60,000 ticks), there is a band in the upper part of the range where both `r1` and `r2` are below the LP's real loss in the moved token. That loss is `amountMoved - amount0 - amount1/price`. Example with a call, a 20% seller ratio and sf = 100 (about 92,000 ticks, e.g. width 1535 at tick spacing 60): when the sqrt price is 30 times the lower bound, the real loss is 94.4% of `amountMoved`, but the function returns `max(r0, r1, r2)` = 91.1%. With sf = 1000 (width 2303 at tick spacing 60), the real loss reaches about 97.6% while the requirement stays near 89.9%. Puts have the same gap near `tickLower`.
  - **Attack sketch:** The attacker picks a strike and a large width so that the current price already sits in the bad band, which makes the leg deep in the money but still in range. The attacker then mints a single-leg short. At mint the attacker is credited the in-the-money amount and only has to post the buffered requirement. Next, the attacker withdraws collateral down to the unbuffered requirement. This only works if the withdrawal check does not use the 1.333 buffer; I could not see the PanopticPool to confirm. The account is now underwater, but the engine still calls it solvent. It cannot be liquidated until the price reaches the edge where `r1` takes over. The shortfall is paid by the PLPs. Even without that withdrawal step, any account that drifts to the maintenance threshold inside this band is liquidated with negative equity, which creates bad debt. Fix: add `required` (the full base) instead of `r0` in `r2`.
  - **Severity:** Medium (High if withdrawals are checked without a buffer)
  - **Confidence:** medium

- **2. Division by zero for a one-tick-wide long leg makes the account's solvency check revert, which blocks liquidation**
  - **Root cause:** in the long branch, line 1487 sets `positionWidth = tickUpper - tickLower`. Line 1489 to 1494 sets `distanceFromStrike = max(positionWidth / 2, |atTick - strike|)`. Line 1522 to 1523 then divides by `(distanceFromStrike * expValue)`. Take a pool with tick spacing 1 and a leg with width 1. `getRangesFromStrike` gives `tickLower = strike` and `tickUpper = strike + 1`, so `positionWidth / 2 == 0`. When `atTick == strike`, the divisor is 0 and the call panics. `unchecked` does not stop a division-by-zero panic. So the leg reverts exactly when it is in range.
  - **Attack sketch:** The attacker uses a pool with tick spacing 1, such as a stablecoin pool or a liquid-staking-token pool. A second account sells one-tick chunks at several ticks next to the current tick. The attacker's main account buys width-1 long legs on those chunks while the spot tick is elsewhere, so the mint check passes. The same account also holds large short positions. When the oracle spot tick lands on any of those strikes, `isAccountSolvent` reverts. Liquidation and force exercise of the account then revert as well. Meanwhile the attacker can still burn the blocking legs, because the check after the burn excludes them. Losses that build up while liquidation is blocked become bad debt. Honest users also cannot mint or keep a one-tick long at the current tick.
  - **Severity:** Medium
  - **Confidence:** medium

- **3. `getSqrtRatioAtTick(tickUpper - tickLower)` reverts for very wide short legs, which makes the account unliquidatable while the price is in range**
  - **Root cause:** line 1476, `Math.getSqrtRatioAtTick((tickUpper - tickLower))`, is not clamped. The ratio inputs on line 1437 to 1450 are clamped with `Math.bound`, but this input is not. `getSqrtRatioAtTick` reverts when the absolute tick is above 887272. Width can be up to 4095, so `width * tickSpacing` exceeds 887272 whenever the tick spacing is 217 or more. Uniswap v4 allows tick spacing up to 32767.
  - **Attack sketch:** On such a pool, the attacker mints a short leg wider than 887272 ticks while the price is outside its range. The in-range branch is skipped, so the mint check passes. The same account holds risky shorts. Once the price moves into the huge range, every solvency evaluation of the account reverts at line 1476. Because the range covers more than half of all ticks, this lasts a long time. Liquidators cannot close the account, and the losses become bad debt for the PLPs.
  - **Severity:** Medium
  - **Confidence:** low (it needs a pool with tick spacing of 217 or more to be supported)

- **4. Short legs are sized with rounded-down amounts, and the requirement for long legs decays to an absolute 10,000 wei**
  - **Root cause:** line 1379 to 1384 calls `getAmountsMoved(..., false)`. For short legs that have a width, this takes the rounding-down branch, so the short requirement uses rounded-down notional. Line 1520 to 1525 adds `TEN_BPS` (10,000) as a raw token amount, not as a share of notional. A long leg's requirement therefore shrinks toward about 10,000 wei once the price is a few widths away from the strike, on either side.
  - **Attack sketch:** The attacker opens very large, narrow long legs far from the price with almost no collateral. If the price gaps into range between oracle updates, the premium owed builds up faster than the near-zero collateral can cover. That unpaid premium is taken out of the sellers' share.
  - **Severity:** Medium
  - **Confidence:** low
