## Summary

The revised proposal splits the work into two papers. Paper 1 compares function bodies written by two current coding agents with the historical developer implementation of the same function in an audited Solidity snapshot. The agents are Claude Code and Codex CLI, with three fresh attempts each. Functions come from a probability sample with two strata. Stratum V holds functions linked to a qualifying High or Medium finding. Stratum N holds the other eligible functions. The core is 15 protocols, with up to two V and eight N functions each. Each implementation gets its own discovery campaign, and that campaign never sees the audit. A flaw counts only after three steps: an executable PoV, a correction to the target body alone, and a decision by two outside adjudicators. The primary endpoint is D = C·L·V_own. It is estimated by a weighted stratified difference with a delete-one-protocol jackknife. It is tested with Holm over the two configurations at a simulated level of 0.04, with calibrated interval and equivalence multipliers. Paper 1 also reports the estimate from V functions alone beside the weighted one. It checks detection in three ways: 60 matched seeded pairs, blind rediscovery of audit flaws, and paid auditors. Paper 2 is a conditional experiment. It varies wrong assertions, permission to run repository tests, and PoV-guided repair, and it has a matched non-security control.

**My iteration-1 weaknesses, one line each.**
1. Common replay gave agent flaws extra chances. **Resolved.** The primary label now comes only from each implementation's own campaign. Replay and audit attacks are secondary, and the primary records are locked before them (5.7, 5.8). The generator draws detection independently for each attempt.
2. Detection could differ by author, and RQ4 was too small to measure it. **Partly resolved.** The matched seeded pairs fix the measurement design, and the coarse precision (half-width 0.135 to 0.194) is stated honestly. But the size is set by the budget, not by a target width. Seeds also cannot measure how an audit filters the flaws left in historical N code (new weakness 3). The overlap between reviewer and generator model families is only reported (new weakness 7).
3. The "human" baseline may be AI-assisted. **Resolved.** Provenance is recorded in three categories, and the baseline is renamed. The subset without AI markers is a preregistered secondary analysis, and the threat is listed in 6.8.
4. The endpoint dropped whenever an agent failed. **Resolved.** D requires functional success, and the reading rule I asked for is in 6.2. How "does not fall" will be tested is still open (question 1).
5. The power model had no protocol-by-author effect and only one h_N value. **Mostly resolved.** The generator now has a protocol-by-author effect (SD 0.5) and two h_N values. In my rerun at SD 1.0, the selected procedure still held its error rate. But power for the +0.10 rows fell from 0.51–0.93 to 0.45–0.83, and the full range is deferred to month two. The proposal declines to state a minimum detectable difference and reports power honestly instead. I accept that.
6. The value of an unresolved attempt was unstated. **Resolved as a rule.** But the rule is missing from the power analysis, where it matters a great deal (new weakness 1).
7. The RQ2 model assumed equal treatment effects for every function. **Resolved.** The primary analysis is now a function-level contrast, averaged within protocols and tested across protocols. That test does not need a common slope. Ceiling rates are deferred to the paper 2 freeze.
8. RQ2 rested on one configuration. **Resolved.** Both configurations are protected, and a claim beyond one configuration needs the same direction in both.
9. The pilot evidence was reported selectively. **Resolved.** Section 3 reports both preregistered contrasts, the 9 of 12 shown-test result, and Codex's 6 of 12 against 6 of 12. It also says the hypothesis came after the pilot. The numbers match the repair study results (`docs/pilot/repair_input_study_results.md`).
10. The strongest agents were only secondary. **Resolved.** Claude Code and Codex CLI with current vendor models form the primary family on the full sample.
11. The protocol frame was built from findings. **Resolved in the text (5.1).** The simulation has not followed: every simulated protocol still has at least two V functions (new weakness 5).
12. Adjudicators knew which code was the human's. **Resolved.** There are two adjudicators from outside the construction team. Packs hide author and arm, and disagreements and blinding failures are recorded.
13. The confirmation workload was not estimated. **Resolved as a plan.** Candidate counts (131 to 242), hours and a 357-hour reserve follow from stated pilot rates and assumptions, and I could recompute each one. No unit cost has been measured yet.

## Strengths

1. **Each implementation now has the same chance of being labelled vulnerable.** Its primary label comes only from its own campaign, under one frozen procedure and budget. That campaign gets no audit material and no attacks from other implementations. Pooled replay and audit-assisted views are reported separately, after the primary records are locked.
2. **The simulation evidence is reported accurately and without overclaiming.** Every quoted number matches its result file. The proposal says plainly what the core cannot do:
   - it cannot reliably detect differences below about 0.07;
   - worst-scenario power stays below 0.80 even for the larger differences;
   - a nonsignificant result does not show equivalence.
3. **The calibrated procedure held up under the variations I tried.** Calibration and validation use separate random streams. The equivalence check uses data generated at true differences of ±0.05. I ran the selected 15-protocol procedure on 6 null scenarios with 400 studies each:
   - with a protocol-by-author SD of 1.0, worst family-wise error was 0.033 and minimum coverage 0.968;
   - with a function SD of 2.5, they were 0.048 and 0.958.
4. **Audit blindness is a sequence that can be checked.** Section 5.4 assigns blind work and audit-informed work according to recorded prior exposure. Labels are sealed with a recorded hash. Blind artifacts are committed with a timestamp before anyone on that protocol opens the audit.
5. **Failed generation cannot pass for security.** D requires functional success, and failed attempts stay in the denominator. The phrase "better security" also requires that the rate of working outputs without a confirmed flaw does not fall.
6. **Costs are written as formulas with stated assumptions.** The candidate counts, the hour subtotals (726 to 1,413, plus 30) and the reserve follow from the stated pilot rates. I checked the arithmetic.

## Weaknesses

1. **The rule for unresolved outcomes decides every directional claim, but the power analysis leaves it out, and it removes most of the power.**
   Problem: Section 6.2 says "A directional claim must survive uncertainty at the adverse bound." Take a claim that agents produce more vulnerable code. For it, the adverse bound sets every unresolved historical outcome to one and every unresolved agent outcome to zero. So each percentage point of unresolved historical outcomes takes about one point off an effect that the power table puts at +0.02 to +0.09. The design has several sources of unresolved outcomes:
   - candidates beyond the equal confirmation limit;
   - severity cases that "remain unresolved" (section 4);
   - harnesses that cannot run.

   The generator (`tools/power_sim_v8.py`) produces no unresolved outcomes at all. So the power table in section 6.3 describes a decision rule that the study will not use.
   Evidence: I copied `study()` from `power_sim_v8.py`. I gave every implementation, of either author, the same probability u of an unresolved security result. Then I tested the adverse lower bound with the selected 15-protocol procedure: Holm level 0.04, 400 studies per row, counting only rejections in the correct direction. Here is the lowest configuration power at each u:

   | Unresolved share u | Larger differences (+0.069 to +0.090) | Smaller differences |
   |---|---|---|
   | 0 | 0.55 to 0.92 (close to the recorded table) | about the recorded values |
   | 0.02 | 0.27 to 0.54 | 0.03 to 0.08 |
   | 0.05 | 0.05 to 0.13 | 0.00 |

   Required change: Rerun the section 6.3 calibration and power analysis with the adverse-bound rule as the decision rule. Use unresolved rates from 0 to 0.10, allowed to differ by author. Add to the month-two launch criteria the largest unresolved rate at which the core keeps useful power.

2. **Paper 2's security-specific contrast θ comes out positive when agents only follow ordinary test obedience.**
   Problem: The outcome in every arm is whether the vulnerability is kept. A wrong security assertion endorses that vulnerability, so an agent that only tries to make its executed tests pass keeps it. A correct security assertion fails on the vulnerable code, so the same agent removes it. An ordinary assertion, wrong or correct, says nothing about the vulnerability. Suppose agents obey both kinds of assertion equally. Then I_security is large, I_ordinary is near zero, and θ is positive. That is the general effect of incorrect tests. Section 6.6 calls that effect "insufficient", and ImpossibleBench already measures it (Zhong et al., ICLR 2026, https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.20270, VERIFIED).
   The simulation hides this. In `contrast_power` (`tools/power_sim_v10.py`, line 99), both assertion types get the same assertion effect (+0.5t) and the same execution effect (−0.5x). If agents simply obey tests, those effects differ by type. The table's alternative "security 1.0, ordinary 0" is exactly what obedience produces with a push of 0.5 on the logit scale in each direction. This is also why my iteration-1 question 3 is still open. That question asked how to tell "wrong tests hurt" apart from "correct tests help".
   Evidence: Section 6.6 says "The primary outcome is retention or reproduction of the same attributable vulnerability. This applies to security-related assertions and ordinary assertions alike". It also says "A wrong ordinary output is not substituted for a vulnerability outcome."
   Required change: Make the primary outcome, for each assertion type, whether the result keeps the behavior that the wrong assertion endorses. For security assertions that is the vulnerability; for ordinary assertions it is the wrong output. Give both types matched contrary evidence: the shown PoV for one, and a failing check derived from the specification for the other. Test θ on that outcome.

3. **No step measures how well the campaign finds the flaws that matter most: flaws in historical N code that survived an audit.**
   Problem: In the simulated frame, stratum N holds about 95% of eligible functions (π_V is about 0.05). So the weighted estimate is mostly an N-stratum comparison. The sensitivity analysis in section 6.5 needs a range for s_HN, the campaign's sensitivity on historical N code. That code has passed a professional audit, so its remaining flaws are the ones the auditors missed. None of the three validation sources measures recall on such flaws:
   - seeded flaws are equally hard in both authors' code, by design;
   - blind rediscovery uses V flaws, which auditors did find;
   - the paid investigation will see few historical N flaws. At the planning rates h_N = 0.02 to 0.05, it will see about one per 20 to 50 historical N implementations it reviews.

   So the s_HN range in section 6.5 will be an assumption.
   Evidence: Section 6.8 says "Human N code has already survived an audit. Seeded flaws may be easier than natural ones." Section 6.5 says "Development data will define plausible scenario ranges before main collection". But no development step covers this class of flaw.
   Required change: Add a small validation set of flaws that an earlier audit of the same code missed and a later review found, for example in a later contest, a mitigation review or a bounty report. Run the blind campaign on the code before the fix, and use its rediscovery rate to set the s_HN range in section 6.5. [uncertain] I do not know how many such cases the pipeline's 19,666 findings contain.

4. **A small design difference may only mean that the blind campaign missed the audit flaws.**
   Problem: With labels from each implementation's own campaign, the historical V-stratum rate is h_V·s_HV. Suppose the blind campaign misses audit flaws in historical V code. Then the V-only estimate moves toward the weighted one, and the design difference shrinks for a reason that has nothing to do with sampling. Section 3 says "A result showing little difference is informative". With these labels, such a result is ambiguous.
   When detection is good, the sign is predictable. V functions are chosen because the historical code failed. When samples are chosen by outcome, the risk difference cannot be estimated (Coston and Kennedy, https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.09016, VERIFIED). So the informative part of the comparison is its size, read together with how well the campaign detects flaws.
   Evidence: The design-comparison results (`tools/power_results_v11.txt`) include a scenario with an agent V rate of 0.9 and recall of 0.8 for agents against 0.5 for historical code. There the true design difference is +0.068 or −0.030, and the calibrated interval excludes zero in at most 7% of studies. In the other 10 scenarios, the true difference is negative, from −0.104 to −0.573.
   Required change: Pre-specify three things for the design comparison:
   - it is reported beside the blind rediscovery rate for historical V flaws (section 5.8);
   - it is also computed with audit-supplemented labels, the third view in section 5.8;
   - a small difference is called informative only when that rediscovery rate is high.

5. **The precision claimed for the design comparison assumes every protocol supplies at least two V functions.**
   Problem: In `tools/power_sim_v8.py`, line 53 reads `NV = 2 + rng.poisson(2, P)`. So every simulated protocol has at least two eligible V functions, and the core always samples 30. The revised frame works differently. It starts from contest listings, excludes findings whose fixes span several function bodies, and allows protocols with no V function (5.1, 5.2). Those protocols never appear in the simulation.
   Evidence: I reran the design-comparison analysis with the same scenarios and the 1.16 multiplier. The only change was that V counts per protocol came from a Poisson distribution that allows zeros. Calibrated coverage stayed at 0.932 to 0.943. The other results:

   | V counts per protocol | V functions sampled | Raw half-width | Rate of excluding zero at a difference of 0.33 | At 0.40 to 0.42 |
   |---|---|---|---|---|
   | As in v8 (at least 2) | 30 | 0.196 to 0.228 | 0.73 | 0.89 to 0.91 |
   | Poisson, mean 1.5 | about 18 | 0.254 to 0.303 | 0.47 | 0.69 to 0.70 |
   | Poisson, mean 1.0 | about 13 | about 0.31 to 0.36 | 0.37 | 0.53 to 0.57 |

   Required change: Before month two, rerun `power_sim_v11.py` with the V counts measured in the frame after all exclusions. State the smallest number of sampled V functions at which section 6.4's statement "the core can show a large design difference" still holds.

6. **The month-one detection gate lets weak procedures pass too often.**
   Problem: The rule is "The campaign must rediscover at least half of the qualifying development vulnerabilities." The candidate cases are the eight pilot findings, and only those that meet the confirmation standard count. A procedure that fails is revised and tried again on fresh cases. Suppose a procedure's true recall is 0.3. With four qualifying flaws, it passes on its first try with probability 0.35. With eight, it passes with probability 0.19. Each revision gives it another chance. The gate also measures recall only on flaws that auditors found, which are not the flaws in weakness 3.
   Evidence: Section 5.9, "Month-one demonstration". The probabilities are exact binomial calculations.
   Required change: Before month one, fix the number of qualifying development flaws (at least six) and the maximum number of revision rounds. Set the threshold so that a procedure with recall 0.3 passes with probability at most 0.1. For example, a threshold of 4 of 6 passes such a procedure with probability 0.07, and 5 of 8 with probability 0.06. The same thresholds pass a procedure with recall 0.7 with probability 0.74 and 0.81.

7. **The LLM reviewers may come from the same model family as the code they judge, and the design only reports this.**
   Problem: Section 5.7 says the reviewers' "relationship to the generator model families is reported." Suppose the two reviewers are a Claude model and a GPT model, as in the pilot. Then each agent's code is judged by one reviewer from its own family, and the historical code is not. LLM judges favor their own outputs (Panickssery et al., NeurIPS 2024, https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.13076, VERIFIED). They also favor the outputs of related models (Li et al., ICLR 2026, https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.01534, VERIFIED). Lower recall on same-family code lowers agent D, which makes agents look safer. The seeded check has 15 pairs for each stratum and configuration, so it cannot detect a moderate difference of this kind.
   Evidence: In the pilot, the reviewers were claude-opus-5-5 and gpt-5.4. The agents used Claude Sonnet 4.5 and GPT-5.4-mini (the agent results for finding 63008, `pilot/tasks/63008/feature_tasks/results.md`).
   Required change: Preregister a secondary analysis that recomputes D for each configuration, on both the agent and the historical implementations, using only fuzzing and the reviewer from the other model family. The confirmation records already keep where each candidate came from, so this needs no new data.

8. **The equivalence margin is large compared with the historical rate.**
   Problem: Equivalence uses margins of −0.05 and +0.05 on D. In the simulated scenarios, the weighted historical D rate is about 0.03 to 0.07. That follows from π_V of about 0.05, h_V of 0.9, h_N of 0.02 or 0.05, and recall of 0.5 to 0.8. So the true agent rate can be about 1.7 to 2.6 times the historical rate and still fall inside the margin. The proposal says the margin "is a planning choice about detected D, not a safety threshold". But readers will take "equivalent" to mean "similar".
   Evidence: Section 6.3, "Calibration and independent validation", and the scenario settings in `tools/power_sim_v10.py`, lines 41 to 50.
   Required change: Set the margin after development, relative to the measured historical D rate (for example, as a ratio of rates), or drop equivalence claims from paper 1.

## Ratings

- C1: concern — The design comparison has a predictable sign, because V functions are chosen for historical failure, and paper 2's θ as defined does not separate security-specific behavior from ordinary test obedience (weaknesses 2 and 4).
- C2: pass — Contracts hold funds, developers report daily AI use, and the proposal claims no AI-caused loss rate.
- C3: pass — Blind discovery for each implementation, function-only attribution, probability weighting and calibrated inference go well beyond applying an LLM to a task.
- C4: concern — The adverse-bound rule decides claims but is missing from the power analysis, and θ comes out positive under ordinary test obedience (weaknesses 1 and 2).
- C5: pass — The attacker, the environment, the assumptions recorded for each protocol, the severity rubric and the exclusions are all explicit.
- C6: concern — RQs, endpoints, baselines and validation are specified, but nothing measures sensitivity on historical flaws that survived an audit, and the design-comparison precision assumes more V functions than the frame may supply (weaknesses 3 and 5).
- C7: concern — The core now has explicit hour assumptions and a 357-hour reserve, but that reserve must also cover running about 1,050 discovery campaigns plus seeded controls, and no unit cost has been measured yet.
- C8: pass — Attacks run offline, disclosure has a 90-day re-check and an embargo, and seeded flaws stay in local copies and are marked in released data.

## Questions for authors

1. How will you test "evidence that the rate of working outputs with no confirmed flaw does not fall": with which margin, at which level, and in which family? In the simulation, agents produce working outputs in 72% of attempts and historical code in 100%. Could the rule ever be met under those assumptions?
2. The estimand is the finite eligible frame. The generator, however, has no step that draws protocols from a frame. Its true value uses superpopulation rates, and the jackknife has no finite-population correction. Will the month-two simulation draw protocols from a finite frame, with the measured sampling fraction and unequal inclusion probabilities for protocols?
3. Section 6.8 repeats the primary analysis "with protocol order as a covariate". The primary estimator is a weighted ratio with a jackknife, not a regression. How will the covariate enter it?
4. Part C assumes a design effect of 1.5 for the 60 seeded pairs. How many distinct seed patterns will you use, and at most how many pairs per pattern? In the pilot, the intraclass correlation across runs of one task was 0.69 (`docs/pilot/pilot_results.md`). At a design effect of 3.1, the 60-pair half-width would be 0.194 to 0.278.
5. Three attempts per configuration mean that 900 of the 1,050 discovery campaigns are agent attempts. Your own generator at 15 protocols gives a raw half-width of 0.0505 with three attempts (1,050 campaigns) and 0.0564 with two (750 campaigns). At 20 protocols with two attempts, it gives 0.0465 with 1,000 campaigns. Will month two choose the number of attempts from precision per measured unit cost?
6. Paper 2's power assumes 160 functions spread over 20 protocols, eight per protocol. Paper 1's frame gives about two to four eligible V functions per protocol. Where will 160 validated vulnerable functions come from? Does power change when they come from more protocols with fewer functions each?
7. In the frame built from contest listings, how many protocols have zero or one qualifying V function after the multi-function exclusion?

## Overall score

The revision fixes most of my iteration-1 concerns, and it reports its evidence with unusual care. Two problems remain. The decision rule for unresolved outcomes could leave the core with little power, and the simulation does not show this. Paper 2's main contrast cannot separate a security-specific effect from ordinary test obedience. Three changes would raise my score to 4:
- the power analysis under the adverse-bound rule, with a launch criterion on the unresolved rate (weakness 1);
- a matched outcome for θ (weakness 2);
- a measurement of campaign sensitivity on historical flaws that survived an audit (weakness 3).
