# Review R1 (SE PC member: ICSE, FSE, ASE), iteration 2

Proposal reviewed: `proposal_workspace/iter_2/proposal.md` (version 11).

## Summary

The revised proposal now plans two papers.

**Paper 1** compares two sets of function bodies inside fixed, audited Solidity snapshots:
- bodies written by Claude Code and Codex CLI;
- the body the historical developer wrote.

The sample is drawn by probability from 15 protocols, or 20 if measured costs allow. Each protocol gives up to two functions linked to a High or Medium audit finding (stratum V) and eight other functions (stratum N). Each agent gets three attempts per function.

Each implementation gets its own discovery campaign, which never sees the audit. The campaign uses Foundry fuzzing and two LLM reviewers. A flaw counts only after three things:
- a PoV shows it;
- a correction to that function alone removes it;
- two independent adjudicators accept it.

The primary endpoint, D, counts implementations that compile, pass the ordinary functional checks, and still contain a confirmed flaw. Paper 1 also compares the estimate from V functions alone with the weighted estimate over the whole population. Detection is checked in three ways: 60 matched seeded pairs, rediscovery of the audit's own findings, and paid auditors.

**Paper 2** depends on time and funding. It asks whether repository assertions that endorse a vulnerability keep that vulnerability alive, during writing and during PoV-guided repair. The comparison is with wrong assertions about ordinary behavior.

Besides the proposal, I checked:

**Status of my iteration-1 weaknesses**

1. Unequal search for flaws: **resolved.** Each implementation's primary label now comes only from its own audit-blind campaign (5.7). Audit-derived attacks appear only in secondary views (5.8), and rediscovery of historical V flaws is reported.
2. AI-assisted baseline: **resolved.** Provenance is recorded in three categories (5.3). The baseline is renamed "historical developer implementation", the threat is listed (6.8), and the subset with no observed assistance is a preregistered secondary analysis.
3. Effect sizes without an empirical basis: **partly resolved.** The equivalence test is now calibrated and power is reported honestly. The study is reframed as an estimation study and no longer promises a significant result. The scenario rates (historical N rate 0.02 or 0.05) are still assumptions. I no longer list this as a separate weakness, but it feeds Weakness 1.
4. Infeasible core: **mostly resolved.** The core is cut to 150 functions and two configurations, with assumed costs and decisions made before outcomes. The remaining gap between hours and timeline is Weakness 3.
5. K counted broken code: **resolved.** D is primary, and `tools/power_sim_v8.py` now generates compilation and functional success.
6. Convenience configurations: **resolved.** Claude Code and Codex CLI with current models run on the full sample.
7. Tests written against the human body steer the agent: **resolved.** Results are split by whether repository tests directly call the target (end of 6.4).
8. Bugs that span several functions: **resolved.** The exclusion rule is fixed before sampling, its share is reported (5.1), and multi-function replacement is an extension.
9. Missing related work: **resolved.** SolEval, Cotroneo et al. and ExecCritic are added with differences in technique. However, the new difference claimed against ImpossibleBench rests on a contrast that I think does not measure what it claims (Weakness 4).
10. No estimate of how common faulty tests are: **resolved.** Section 6.7 estimates the weighted share of V functions with semantically confirmed natural faulty tests.
11. Selective pilot reporting: **resolved.** Section 3 now gives both null preregistered contrasts and the Codex 6 of 12 against 6 of 12 result. It also says that no person has reviewed the repairs and that the hypothesis came after the pilot.
12. Too much for one paper: **resolved.** There are now two papers with separate freezes (Section 10).

## Strengths

1. **The primary comparison is now fair by construction.** Every implementation, historical or agent, is labeled only by its own campaign. That campaign sees no audit report, audit PoV or historical correction. Primary records are locked before any pooled or audit-assisted replay. Four views are reported, so a reader can see what audit knowledge and pooled search each add (5.8).
2. **The statistics are honest and they match the code.**
   - Inference is calibrated on one random stream and validated on an independent one.
   - The equivalence check uses data generated at the true ±0.05 boundary.
   - The proposal says plainly what the core cannot detect: "The core cannot reliably detect the smaller differences" (6.3) and "the core can show a large design difference, but not a moderate one" (6.4).
3. **The rules for building tasks without audit knowledge are concrete and checkable.**
   - Labels are sealed with a recorded hash.
   - The blind artifacts are committed before anyone opens the audit.
   - Prior exposure is recorded, and a different person builds the blind artifacts for protocols the student already knows (5.4).

   SE studies of generated code rarely state this. It also makes the resulting benchmark reusable.
4. **The rules for counting outcomes set a good standard for the SE community.**
   - Failed generations stay in the denominator.
   - Unresolved outcomes get admissible bounds, and a directional claim must survive the bound that is worst for it (6.2).
   - "Better security" may not be claimed when working completion falls.
5. **The scope now fits a first-year student, given the gates.** Expansion to 20 protocols, four V functions, paper 2, or more seeded pairs each needs measured costs. Each decision is fixed before outcomes (8).
6. **The released artifacts would be useful.** They include threat-model records, blind properties, functional suites, PoVs and seeded flaws for real DeFi functions. Neither the SE nor the security community has such a set for Solidity.

## Weaknesses

1. **The comparison that the proposal calls paper 1's contribution is a secondary analysis, and the plan claims more for a small result than the precision allows.**
   - **Problem.** Section 3 presents "compare both sampling designs on the same data" as paper 1's key insight. Section 6.3 ends: "The study is justified as an estimation and design-comparison study." Yet Section 6.3 also says "the comparison between sampling designs [is] secondary unless separately included in the preregistered multiplicity plan." The only primary test is D for the two configurations. At 15 protocols its minimum power is 0.13 to 0.38 for true differences of +0.022 to +0.042 (`tools/power_results_v10_a.txt`). The likely paper therefore has an inconclusive primary test, and its headline comes from a secondary analysis.
   - **Evidence.** Section 3 says "A result showing little difference is informative." The calibrated half-width for the difference between the V-only and weighted estimates is 0.252 at 15 protocols (`tools/power_results_v11_calib.txt`). An estimate near zero with that half-width does not show that the difference is small.
   - **Required change:** Add the difference between the V-only and weighted D estimates, for each configuration, to the preregistered multiplicity plan as a co-primary estimand. State the smallest difference that would matter to someone building a benchmark. Describe a result as "little difference" only if the calibrated interval excludes that size.

2. **Public audit reports and public fixes exist only for V functions, which can bias exactly the comparison paper 1 is built on.**
   - **Problem.** A V function has a public audit report that describes its flaw and a public fix commit. An N function has neither. If a model's training data contain the report or the fix, the agent can avoid the historical bug in V functions without any security reasoning. That makes agents look better in V only, and so it inflates the gap between the V-only and weighted estimates.
   - **Evidence.**
     - The proposal treats exposure as if it were the same in both strata. Section 6.8 says "Public code or fixes may have appeared in training. Dates and trajectory similarity cannot prove non-exposure." Section 5.3 says "A report published after a model cutoff does not establish that the code was unseen."
     - That reasoning holds for the code, which is public in both strata. It does not hold for the report and the fix: if they appeared after a model's cutoff, the model cannot have trained on them. Section 5.3 already records these dates, but no analysis uses them.
     - Al-Kaswan et al. (MSR 2026, https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.10496) show that exposure to the buggy line raises bug reproduction. Liang et al. (ICSE-SEIP 2026, https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.12286) show that models recall SWE-bench files.
     - In the pilot, two configurations wrote "the same line as the human fix `c01b6c7`" (`pilot/tasks/63008/feature_tasks/results.md`). The pilot cannot tell whether this came from reasoning or from recall.
   - **Required change:** For each V function, record whether its audit report and fix commit were public before each pinned model's training cutoff. Preregister the V-stratum D difference, and the comparison between the V-only and weighted estimates, separately for the functions whose report and fix appeared after both cutoffs. At month two, report how many such functions the frame contains.

3. **The hour budget does not fit the timeline at the pessimistic rates, and running the agents and campaigns has no row of its own.**
   - **Problem and evidence.**
     - The annual ceiling of 1,800 student hours is 150 hours a month. Section 10 gives months 2 to 5 to construction, about 600 hours, and month 2 also holds the budget and frame work.
     - The construction rows of the planning table add up to 450 to 840 hours at 15 protocols: protocol preparation 90 to 180, V tasks 120 to 240, N tasks 180 to 360, and seeds 60. At the pessimistic end, construction overruns its months by about 240 hours.
     - The 0.5 hours per seeded artifact also looks low, because each seed must "produce an attributable violation under the same threat model" (5.9).
     - Running 900 generation attempts and 1,050 discovery campaigns (F + F×2×3 with F = 150) has no row in the table. "Campaign setup and supervision, replay operation" draw on the 357-hour reserve. The same reserve also pays for the month-one development cases and the 63008 evidence package.
     - At the pessimistic rates, paper 1 uses the whole year: 1,443 hours plus the 357-hour reserve equals 1,800. Paper 2 then has no student hours, although Section 8 says it has "separate resource allocations."
     - The 1,800-hour ceiling also seems to assume no coursework or teaching [uncertain].
   - **Required change:** Add a month-by-month table of student hours. It should place every row of the planning table, a separate row for running generation, discovery and replay, and the month-one development work against 150 hours per month. It should also state paper 2's student hours, writing zero if there are none.

4. **Paper 2's security-specific contrast would come out positive for any agent that fits its tests, so it cannot show a security-specific effect.**
   - **Problem.** RQ2 asks whether assertions that endorse a vulnerability preserve it "beyond the effect of matched incorrect non-security assertions." Every arm measures one outcome: whether the vulnerability is retained (6.6).
     - A wrong assertion about ordinary behavior says nothing about the vulnerable behavior. Its effect on vulnerability retention should be near zero, whatever the agent thinks about security.
     - So θ = I_security − I_ordinary is about I_security. It will be positive for any agent that simply fits the tests it runs.
     - In the repair arms, the security arm has a shown PoV that contradicts the wrong assertion. The ordinary arm has no shown test that contradicts its wrong assertion. So the arms differ in the conflicting evidence as well as in security.
   - **Evidence.** The pilot already shows agents fitting the tests they run. OpenHands "fitted its change to the shown test and to the human's tests at the same time" (`pilot/tasks/63008/feature_tasks/results.md`). A positive θ would repeat that finding. It would not show that the exploitable consequence changes agent behavior, and that is the question that matters to SE.
   - **Required change:** Make the ordinary arm mirror the security arm. Use a wrong repository assertion about a behavior of the same function that cannot be exploited, contradicted by a shown failing test of that behavior. In both arms, make the primary outcome whether the final code satisfies the wrong assertion or the shown test.

5. **The LLM reviewers may come from the same model families as the agents they review, and the design only reports this.**
   - **Problem.** Section 5.7 uses "the same two LLM reviewers" and says only that "their relationship to the generator model families is reported." If the reviewers are a Claude model and a GPT model, as in the pilot, each agent's code is reviewed by one reviewer from its own family. The historical code has no such relation to either reviewer.
   - **Evidence.**
     - LLM judges favor their own outputs (Panickssery et al., NeurIPS 2024, https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.13076) and the outputs of related models (Li et al., ICLR 2026, https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.01534).
     - The two pilot reviewers differed greatly in recall: "Claude Opus 5.5 reported all seven… GPT-6.1 Sol reported two" (`docs/pilot/repair_input_study_results.md`, Section 6).
     - Executable confirmation removes false reports. It does not recover flaws that a reviewer misses because of family preference. Such misses would bias D differently for each configuration and could reverse their order.
   - **Required change:** Record which reviewer produced each confirmed candidate. Preregister a secondary D analysis that compares each configuration with the historical code using fuzzing plus only the reviewer that is not from that configuration's model family.

6. **Who writes the request is not controlled, although the pilot shows that the request decides the outcome.**
   - **Problem.**
     - The student writes requests for protocols new to them. The second person writes requests for protocols the student already knows (5.4).
     - Requests come from "permitted documentation." There is no template, no length limit, and no rule on how much boundary behavior to specify.
     - The request diagnostic is applied after writing, and results are split by its classification. But the classification itself depends on who wrote the request.
     - Section 6.8 records builder identity but adjusts only for protocol order.
   - **Evidence.** In the pilot, all 13 compiled runs whose request described the human's design contained the bug, against 3 of 15 whose request stated the fixed rule (Fisher p = 0.00002; `docs/pilot/pilot_results.md`, Section 5.11). Because the builder changes with the protocol, differences between builders' writing styles are mixed up with differences between protocols in D.
   - **Required change:** Freeze a written request template before sampling. For a random subset of about 15 sampled functions, have both builders write requests independently. Report how often the two requests receive the same classification, and add builder as a covariate in a secondary analysis.

7. **The month-one launch gate is too small to separate a weak campaign from an adequate one.**
   - **Problem.** The campaign "must rediscover at least half of the qualifying development vulnerabilities" (5.9). The candidates are the eight pilot findings, and only those that meet the confirmation standard count. If the campaign fails, it is revised and rerun on "fresh development cases," but the size of that reserve is never given.
   - **Evidence.** By the binomial distribution with eight cases, a campaign with true recall 0.3 passes about 19% of the time. A campaign with true recall 0.5 fails about 36% of the time. Repeated revisions on small fresh sets make passing more likely without making the campaign better.
   - **Required change:** Fix the number of development vulnerabilities in the reserve before month one, taken from protocols outside the sampling frame. Report the gate's probability of passing at true recall 0.3 and 0.5 for that number.

8. **The simulation for the V-only against weighted comparison assumes every protocol has V functions, and the rule for choosing four V functions per protocol depends on a quantity the development cases cannot estimate.**
   - **Problem.**
     - `tools/power_sim_v8.py` draws `NV = 2 + rng.poisson(2, P)`, so every simulated protocol has at least two V functions.
     - Sections 5.1 and 5.2 start the frame from contest listings where possible, and allow protocols with no V functions. Each such protocol adds nothing to the V-only estimate, so the reported 0.252 half-width is optimistic.
     - Section 6.4 considers four V functions only "if development cases put the expected design difference between about 0.15 and 0.35." Three development cases, with at most a few V functions each, cannot estimate that quantity.
   - **Required change:** Rerun `tools/power_sim_v11.py` using the share of protocols without V functions measured in the refreshed frame. Choose two or four V functions per protocol from that rerun and the measured extra cost alone, and drop the condition on the development cases' estimated difference.

## Ratings

- C1: concern — The measurement design is new: no verified paper weights unflagged functions and uses executable evidence. But the V-only against weighted comparison, which carries paper 1's novelty, is secondary in the analysis plan, and paper 2's contrast as designed would show test fitting, not a security effect (Weaknesses 1 and 4).
- C2: pass — The problem is real and backed by survey and loss evidence, D now matches the question practitioners ask, and the V-only against weighted result would inform benchmark builders.
- C3: pass — Building tasks without audit knowledge, labeling each implementation by its own campaign, attributing flaws to one function, and calibrated weighted inference go well beyond "apply an LLM to task X."
- C4: concern — The assumptions of the main comparison are now stated, but public reports and fixes for V functions only, and reviewers from the agents' own model families, can bias the key comparisons (Weaknesses 2 and 5).
- C5: pass — Attacker capabilities, environment, severity rubric and exclusions are explicit and recorded per protocol.
- C6: concern — Datasets, baselines, metrics, ablations and threats are named, but the request writer, the reviewer family, the launch gate and protocols without V functions are neither controlled nor simulated (Weaknesses 5 to 8).
- C7: concern — The smaller core with cost gates is plausible, but at pessimistic rates the hour table does not fit the construction months and leaves running the agents and campaigns to the reserve (Weakness 3).
- C8: pass — Offline execution, disclosure with a 90-day re-check, an embargo, seeded flaws kept to local copies, and an institutional review check are all stated.

## Questions for authors

1. Of the 43 findings with public history, how many have a fix confined to one function body? I asked this in iteration 1, and it can be counted now.
2. Which models will be the two primary reviewers? Will either share a family with Claude Code or Codex CLI?
3. Section 3 reports contrast (a) as "adding the shown PoV: 0 of 12 successes versus 2 of 12." In the repair study, both arms of that contrast had a full terminal (L-E2 against P-E2). Will you say so, since paper 2 removes the full terminal?
4. The subtotal "726 to 1,413, plus 30" in the planning table is the sum of its rows, 756 to 1,443, written in an unusual way. Will you give the sum directly?
5. How are the "recall flags" in Section 6.8 defined? Which threshold triggers the secondary exclusion analysis?
6. Where would paper 2's 160 validated vulnerable functions come from, when paper 1's core has 30 V functions?
7. Does the 1,800-hour annual ceiling assume no coursework or teaching duties?
8. Which audit platforms make up the contest-listing frame? What share of listed protocols do you expect to have no qualifying finding?

## Overall score

Score: 3

The revision fixes the soundness and scope problems that led to my earlier score of 2. Three required changes would raise my score to 4:
- make the V-only against weighted comparison co-primary, and stop calling a small, imprecise result informative (Weakness 1);
- analyze V functions whose audit report and fix appeared after the models' training cutoffs (Weakness 2);
- reconcile the hour budget with the timeline month by month (Weakness 3).
