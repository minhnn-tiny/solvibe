# Review R1 (SE PC member: ICSE, FSE, ASE), iteration 3

Proposal reviewed: `proposal_workspace/iter_3/proposal.md` (version 15).

## Summary

The proposal now plans one protected study and one extension that has no student hours.

The study replaces one function body at a time in audited Solidity snapshots. Claude Code and Codex CLI each write two attempts per function, and the historical developer body is the baseline. Twenty protocols are drawn at random. Each one gives up to six functions linked to a High or Medium finding (stratum V) and eight other functions (stratum N).

A function enters the primary population only if its task passes a fixed construction checklist within that function's own time allowance. This is decided before any agent runs. Each implementation gets its own audit-blind discovery campaign, made of Foundry fuzzing and two LLM reviewers. A flaw counts only when three things hold:
- a PoV shows it;
- a correction to that function body alone removes it;
- two independent adjudicators accept it.

The endpoint D counts implementations that compile, pass the ordinary functional checks and contain a confirmed flaw.

The principal estimand, RQ1a, is the V-only agent-minus-historical difference in D minus the domain-weighted difference. Its meaningful-difference threshold is 0.10. RQ1b is the domain-weighted comparison itself. Detection is checked four ways: 60 matched seeded pairs, rediscovery of audit findings, flaws that an earlier audit missed, and paid auditors.

## Strengths

1. **Construction cannot be used to drop hard cases after the fact.** Section 5.2 decides B_i for each function, within that function's own allowance, before generation. If a promised procedure is not supplied, that is recorded as a protocol deviation, "not relabeled as B_i = 0". Easy tasks cannot fund extra work on hard ones. This is the kind of rule an ICSE reviewer looks for and rarely finds in studies of generated code.
2. **The estimator matches the target.** Constructible stratum shares are estimated from weighted built counts, and they are recomputed in every jackknife replicate (6.3). Undefined replicates "cannot be silently dropped". Both identification bounds enter one full interval (V19, V20).
3. **The 63008 package now shows the attribution standard working end to end.** A one-line body change keeps empty-range behaviour. The fixed-state tests show the honest provider paid and backed. The independent H-02 fix leaves the attack in place. The runner matched all 12 expected outcomes (`logs/v2_summary.log`).
4. **My iteration-1 question is answered with checkable evidence.** In the fix-scope record, 17 of 43 findings (17 of 34 with an identified fix) have a one-body fix. Judgment calls move this between 15 and 18, and Panoptic supplies 9 of the 17. The README states its own limits.
5. **The limits are reported plainly.** The proposal says that V20 assumes construction is independent of outcomes, that Monte Carlo error can decide a pass near 0.80, that no human timing exists, and that "These requirements are not yet met." The file hashes in the manifest (`tools/MANIFEST.md`) make the planning evidence reproducible.

## Weaknesses

1. **By the proposal's own unit rates, the fixed minimum study does not fit its 45-hour per-protocol cap, even at the optimistic end of every rate.**
   - **Problem.** Section 8 says the reservation "must cover the maximum sampled allocation" of 6 V and 8 N functions within 45 hours. The assumed rates in section 8 are: protocol preparation 6–12 hours, V construction 4–8, N construction 1.5–3, confirmation 0.5–1.5 per candidate, and campaign operation 0.1–0.2. At the optimistic end of each, a full protocol costs:
     - preparation, 6 hours;
     - construction, 6×4 + 8×1.5 = 36 hours;
     - operation of 70 campaigns, 7 hours.

     That is already 49 hours, before generation, confirmation or secondary work. Confirmation at the low candidate rate (70 × 8/64 candidates at 0.5 hours each) adds about 4 more, for about 53 hours. At the pessimistic end the total is about 122 hours.
   - **Evidence.**
     - The proposal's own averages show the same gap: "At the optimistic end of every assumed rate it costs about 43 hours; at the pessimistic end, about 100 hours."
     - At the midpoint of the rates, the average protocol costs about 70 hours. Twenty protocols then need about 1,400 of the 900 available hours.
     - If the minimum does not fit, "the design returns for revision before collection", and there is no pre-reviewed alternative. The most likely outcome of month 2 is therefore no launch.
     - Cheaper allocations exist. In my run, cutting N functions from 8 to 4 per protocol raised the largest raw RQ1a half-width only from 0.146 to 0.154. It raised the largest raw RQ1b half-width from 0.055 to 0.075. At optimistic rates the cut saves about 9 hours per protocol.
   - **Required change:** Replace the single allocation with a short preregistered table of allocations. For each allocation, give its full-allocation cost at the low, middle and high assumed rates, and its RQ1a and RQ1b precision from `tools/power_sim_v20.py`. Add a rule, fixed now, that picks the allocation from the month-1 measured unit costs alone, before sampling.

2. **Under the only measured distribution of V functions, the fixed allocation fails the RQ1a criterion by a wide margin.**
   - **Problem.** The criterion asks for a largest mean full-interval half-width of at most 0.20, and for an interval wholly below −0.10 in at least 0.80 of studies when the true difference is near −0.33. These are met only under the "earlier V assumption" (2 + Poisson(2) per protocol). Under that assumption every protocol has at least two V functions.
   - **Evidence.**
     - V19 with the counted spread, at 20 protocols and no unresolved outcomes, gives a half-width of 0.314 and an exclusion rate of 0.38 (`tools/power_results_v19.txt`).
     - The counted spread [9, 2, 2, 0, 1, 1, 1, 1, 0, 0] is the number of one-body fixes per protocol in the fix-scope README.
     - Two of the frame rules push toward fewer V functions. The fix-scope exclusion keeps only about half of identified fixes (17 of 34). A frame built from contest listings also admits protocols with no qualifying finding.
     - The count could also be larger. The same README lists 36 High and Medium findings for Ammplify, but only one of them entered the pilot table.
     - Only a count can settle this, and the count does not need the student. The automated classification of 43 findings took 864 seconds (`evidence/timings.md`).
   - **Required change:** Before acceptance, count the eligible one-body V functions per protocol in the refreshed frame, using the procedure in `evidence/frame_count/`. Then rerun V19 and V20 on that distribution.

3. **Functional checks written from the audited documentation can endorse the flaw. The flaw then cannot be attributed, which pulls the V difference toward zero.**
   - **Problem.**
     - Builders work from "permitted documentation, signatures, callers and surrounding code" (5.4).
     - Checks are "classified before generation" (5.5).
     - A counted flaw needs a correction that preserves "required functionality under the acceptance checks" (section 4, *Attribution*).
     - Suppose the documentation describes the vulnerable behaviour. A blind builder will then write a functional check that asserts it. The correct one-body correction fails that check, so the historical flaw cannot be counted. A correct agent body also fails L. Both effects shrink the V difference, which is the term RQ1a depends on.
     - The rule in 5.5, "Assertions endorsing known faulty behavior are not accepted as correctness checks", cannot be applied by a builder who has not seen the audit.
   - **Evidence.**
     - In 63008, four audited repository tests assert the faulty exclusion of the start tick. The fix commit `c01b6c7` also edited the library `@dev` invariant note and the `initDown` `@param` notes (`evidence/63008/README.md`).
     - The pilot report says that "the repository's own tests and a code comment describe the buggy behaviour" (`docs/pilot/pilot_results.md`, entry for 63008).
   - **Required change:** Preregister a rule for the case where a correction removes a confirmed violation but fails a frozen functional check. Adjudicators who are blind to the author decide whether the check asserts the attacker outcome. If it does, the check is dropped for every implementation of that function, L is recomputed for both authors, and the number of such cases is reported by stratum.

4. **The study cannot conclude "little difference". In half of the planning scenarios it is built to return "inconclusive".**
   - **Problem.** Section 3 offers three outcomes. One is "little difference", which requires "the full uncertainty interval [to lie] inside ±0.10". That needs a half-width below 0.10. V19 and V20 report largest mean half-widths of 0.163 to 0.223.
   - **Evidence.**
     - In my run, the share of studies whose interval lay inside ±0.10 was 0.00 in all 12 planning scenarios. This includes the scenarios whose true design difference is −0.03 and +0.07.
     - The 12 true values that `power_sim_v8.py` gives are −0.57, −0.50, −0.47, −0.41, −0.40 and −0.33, where V20 reports exclusion rates, and −0.26, −0.19, −0.18, −0.10, −0.03 and +0.07.
     - In the second group of six, my run gave rates between 0.00 and 0.49 for the interval lying wholly below −0.10.
     - So the only reachable conclusions are "large difference" and "inconclusive". Which one comes out depends on whether agents reproduce V flaws at rate 0.6 or 0.9, and on the recall assumptions.
   - **Required change:** State in sections 3 and 6.4 that the planned precision cannot support a "little difference" conclusion. For each planning scenario, report the simulated probability of a "large difference" result and of an "inconclusive" result.

5. **The size of the design difference depends strongly on a recall gap between authors, and the sensitivity analysis does not cover it.**
   - **Problem.** In `power_sim_v8.py`, the detected V difference is aV × 0.72 × s_AV − 0.9 × s_HV. Take an agent V rate of 0.6. The latent V difference is then the same, −0.47, in all three recall settings. The detected design difference changes with recall alone:
     - −0.33 when both recalls are 0.7;
     - −0.10 when agent recall is 0.8 and historical recall is 0.5;
     - −0.50 in the reverse case.
   - **Evidence.**
     - Section 6.5 defines the recall sensitivity analysis for the author difference Δ_latent,B only.
     - The RQ1a reading rule checks only historical V rediscovery ("the lower 95% rediscovery bound is at least 0.5").
     - The seeded pairs bound the recall difference only to a half-width of 0.135–0.194 (5.9).
     - RQ1a is a statement about size against a 0.10 threshold. A recall gap that the pairs cannot rule out can move it across that threshold.
   - **Required change:** Extend the section 6.5 sensitivity analysis to the design difference, with separate agent and historical recall in each stratum. Report which RQ1a conclusions hold over the full feasible recall range, and which hold only under a stated transfer assumption.

6. **The question practitioners ask, RQ1b, has no precision target in the readiness decision.**
   - **Problem.**
     - In the planning model the V share of the frame is about 0.05. The weighted comparison is therefore close to the N-stratum difference d_N. The design difference is close to d_V − d_N.
     - In the six scenarios where RQ1a can succeed, it mostly restates d_V, the flagged-only gap.
     - Existing benchmarks already measure that gap in other languages. SecureVibeBench reports that Claude Code and Codex CLI wrote correct code that still contained the human's PoV-confirmed vulnerability in 26.7% and 21.8% of tasks (VERIFIED in the literature review; https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.22097).
     - The new information is whether agents add exploitable flaws to ordinary functions that the developer wrote correctly. That is RQ1b, and it is the project's own headline question.
   - **Evidence.**
     - Readiness requires only that "the RQ1a criterion [passes]" and "independently checked inference for any RQ1b directional claims" (8).
     - At 20 protocols, V17 gives power of only 0.19–0.47 for a difference of −0.05.
     - V20's plain intervals cover 0.925–0.935, and "V20 does not calibrate its null error for the constructible domain."
   - **Required change:** Add an RQ1b precision target to the readiness decision. State it as the smallest agent excess in D on ordinary functions that would matter, for example +0.05, and report what each allocation in Weakness 1 achieves for it.

## Ratings

- C1: concern — The measurement design is new among verified work: executable, attribution-checked labels from audit-blind campaigns, over a probability sample that includes unflagged functions. But where the principal estimand can succeed it mostly restates the flagged-only gap. Bias from sampling only linked fixes is a known SE concern (Bird et al., ESEC/FSE 2009, https://www.cs.ucdavis.edu/~filkov/papers/biasbusters.pdf) (Weaknesses 4 and 6).
- C2: pass — Survey and loss evidence show the problem is real, and both the flagged-only distortion and the comparison on ordinary functions matter to benchmark builders and developers.
- C3: pass — Per-function construction decided before outcomes, labels from each implementation's own campaign, body-only attribution, and domain shares estimated in every replicate go well beyond applying an LLM to a task.
- C4: concern — Functional checks written from audited documentation can block attribution (Weakness 3), and the RQ1a size depends on a recall gap that no analysis bounds (Weakness 5).
- C5: pass — Attacker capital, ordering, oracle behaviour, severity and exclusions are explicit and recorded for each protocol.
- C6: concern — Data, baselines, metrics, validation and threats are named, but the allocation fails its criterion under the only measured V distribution, "little difference" cannot be concluded, and RQ1b has no precision target (Weaknesses 2, 4 and 6).
- C7: fail — By the proposal's own unit rates, the full 6 V and 8 N reservation exceeds the 45-hour cap even at the optimistic end, no human-timed case exists, and the student's participation is not confirmed (Weakness 1).
- C8: pass — The plan includes offline execution, disclosure with a 90-day re-check and embargo, seeds kept local, and an institutional review check.

## Questions for authors

1. How is "meaningful computation or state changes" applied when deciding which N functions are eligible? Can a script apply it, so that the frame can be reproduced?
2. How many V functions in the refreshed frame have both their report and their fix published after the documented cutoffs of the models you expect to pin? If that number is close to zero, will you deliberately sample recent contests?
3. Will you run generation and campaigns on the second request for the 15 duplicated functions? The pilot's main finding was that "the feature request decided the outcome". Agreement on classification alone does not show how much D changes between builders.
4. Will you report RQ1a for K and for the working-without-confirmed-flaw endpoint? In the planning model, agents compile with probability 0.9 and pass with 0.8, so part of the design difference in D comes from functional failure.
5. What unit values do you assume for h_generation and h_secondary in the section 8 inequality?
6. The gate reserve includes pilot material, and the pilot already ran both LLM reviewers on 63008 repairs (`docs/pilot/repair_input_study_results.md`, section 6). Will the first gate use only flaws that played no part in designing the campaign prompts?
7. Which anchor do you consider more plausible for the agents' V rate? The pilot found the bug in 13 of 13 compiled runs whose request described the human's design. SecureVibeBench reports 21.8% to 26.7%. The answer decides whether RQ1a is likely to be conclusive.
8. Two questions from iteration 2 are still open. Which audit platforms make up the contest-listing frame? What threshold for the recall flag triggers the secondary exclusion analysis?

## Open questions

**OQ1 (C7): must the measured human development record exist before acceptance?**

No. At the proposal stage it is normal to schedule timed development cases in month 1 and to make them a binding launch gate, as section 8 already does. Requiring them earlier would not change the design.

But a conditional acceptance is sensible only if the condition can plausibly be met. Two checks that do not need the student currently say it cannot be met:
- by the proposal's own rates, the full allocation exceeds the 45-hour cap (Weakness 1);
- under the only measured V distribution, the allocation fails the RQ1a criterion (Weakness 2).

Both can be fixed now with arithmetic, an automated frame count and the existing simulators.

A conditional acceptance also needs someone who will meet the condition. The student's commitment to do the month-1 timed cases should be confirmed, with a date, before acceptance.

My answer is therefore: accept conditionally once Weaknesses 1 and 2 are addressed and the student's participation is confirmed. Keep the human timings as the month-1 launch gate, as written.
